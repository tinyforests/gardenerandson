Country

This garden sits on the unceded lands of the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung people of the Kulin Nation. We acknowledge their Elders past and present, and their continuing care for Country. This design seeks to return locally indigenous plants — and the life they carry — to this small piece of it.

The Proposal

A layered indigenous garden that retains the established canopy and existing circulation while renewing the lower planting — strengthening habitat for small birds and pollinators, bringing seasonal colour and foliage contrast, and staying practical for gradual, owner-led installation.

Five connected beds · One front garden

The whole idea in one line: keep what's working, rebuild what's missing underneath.

  • The established trees and the existing paths all stay.
  • The lower layers are what we renew: the shrubs, grasses, flowers and groundcovers.
  • It's designed for you to plant gradually, not a one-day install. It can grow in stages.

Five beds, but read as one front garden.

Site Ecology

Find My Ecological Garden identifies this site as Valley Heathy Forest — a low eucalypt forest of boxes and stringybarks over a dense heathy understorey of peas, cassinias and small shrubs, woven through with wallaby-grasses, sedges and seasonal herbs.

Design response · Keep the canopy, rebuild the missing lower layers

Find My Ecological Garden puts this site in Valley Heathy Forest. That's what grew here before the suburb.

Picture boxes and stringybarks over a thick, heathy shrub layer of peas and small shrubs, with grasses and seasonal flowers through the ground.

The key point is that you still have much of the canopy. What's gone is the understorey, and that's where the small birds and insects actually live. So the design keeps the canopy and rebuilds the missing layers beneath it.

Continuous Front Woodland & Habitat Bed

Walk through it in three layers:

  • Deep in the bed: taller habitat shrubs such as Sweet Bursaria, Snowy Daisy Bush and Austral Indigo. The Bursaria flowers in summer, and that's a big deal for insects.
  • Around the Banksia: dense small-bird cover. Prickly Currant-bush is prickly on purpose, as a safe refuge from cats and bigger birds.
  • At the driveway and footpath edge: low tussocks, bluebells, everlastings and Creeping Bossiaea, which keep sightlines clear.

Banksia 'Birthday Candles' is in here too, flowering autumn through spring.

How the Five Beds Work Together
ZoneEcological rolePriorityStrength
01 · Front Woodland Primary habitat backbone — shelter, nectar, seed, insect habitat and connected ground cover beneath the retained canopy. Retain leaf litter and occasional open ground rather than covering every surface. 9 / 10
02 · Northern Woodland Cool, sheltered woodland edge for shade-tolerant invertebrates and small insect-eating birds. Keep dense or prickly branching for safe bird refuge and potential nesting. 8.5 / 10
03 · Southern Meadow Pollinator and seed-producing meadow layer — seasonal flowers, grasses and movement in the brighter exposure. Plant repeated colonies of grasses and forbs so it functions as a meadow, not isolated specimens. 8 / 10
04 · House-front Understorey Extends flowering continuity — nectar, aromatic foliage and low habitat close to the house. Include evergreen density; retain selected stems and seedheads through their useful season. 7.5 / 10
05 · Arrival Garden An ecological stepping stone — nectar, berries, flowers and soil cover while keeping access and sightlines. Robust, non-prickly planting beside dog and pedestrian routes; Zones 1–2 provide the secure refuge. 7 / 10

This is how it adds up. The scores are our assessment of each bed's habitat value, out of ten.

Zone 1 is the strongest at 9. The Arrival Garden sits at 7 by design, because it's doing a different job.

The priority column is where the difference gets made. It's the small decisions: leaving leaf litter, keeping the prickly cover, planting in colonies, leaving seedheads standing.

A Year of Habitat

Winter nectar

Banksia · Correa · Philotheca carry the garden's food supply through the cold months, when nectar is scarcest.

Spring abundance

Indigofera · Olearia · Wahlenbergia and the flowering groundcovers bring the peak of colour and pollinator activity.

Summer resources

Sweet Bursaria and the meadow flowers sustain insects and small birds through the dry season.

Fruit & seed

Dianella, the grasses and retained mature vegetation feed seed-eaters into autumn — with shelter concentrated in the layered woodland of Zones 1 and 2, and continuous lower planting linking all five beds.

The goal is food all year round, not just a spring show.

  • Winter is the hungry season for nectar. Banksia, Correa and waxflower carry it.
  • Spring is the peak of colour and pollinators.
  • Summer is Sweet Bursaria and the meadow flowers.
  • Autumn is fruit and seed: Dianella berries and the grass seed.

Shelter concentrates in Zones 1 and 2, and the lower planting links all five beds.

Habitat-building Actions
  1. Prioritise Forest Hill–local indigenous provenance when suitable stock is available.
  2. Plant grasses, bluebells, herbs and groundcovers in repeated colonies.
  3. Retain some leaf litter, seedheads, hollow stems and small areas of exposed soil.
  4. Introduce selected logs and rocks for shelter and varied microclimates.
  5. Provide a shallow, safely positioned water source.
  6. Avoid insecticides and minimise unnecessary night lighting.
  7. Maintain clear paths, driveway sightlines, fence access and dog-safe edges.

These are the small things that turn planting into habitat. Pick the ones that matter to Kate rather than reading all seven.

  • Local provenance means Forest Hill stock where we can get it.
  • A bit of mess is good. Leaf litter, seedheads and small bare patches, which many native bees nest in.
  • Logs, rocks and a shallow water dish go somewhere safe.
  • No insecticides, and less night lighting.

And the practical side: paths, driveway sightlines, fence access and dog-safe edges all stay.
