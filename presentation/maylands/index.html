Gardener & Son

The Maylands Meadow

A garden design for
7 Maylands Avenue, Balwyn North
Prepared for Evelyn McKibbon
September 2026

Welcome Evelyn. Short version of the session: what you showed us, what we heard, the garden we've designed, and how it lives through the year.

The name — The Maylands Meadow — is a working title. Invite her to rename it.

Acknowledgement of Country
This garden grows on Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Country.

We acknowledge the Traditional Custodians of the land at Balwyn North and pay our respects to Elders past and present. Every garden we make is a small part of a much older landscape.

Keep it brief and sincere. Pause before moving on.

The idea
A garden that moves, flowers in drifts, and stands through winter.

Grasses hold the frame

Three grasses give the garden its structure and its movement. They're what you'll see swaying from the window.

Flowers come in drifts

Violet, blue and deep burgundy repeat across the beds in loose groups, so the eye travels the whole garden.

Nothing is tidied away

Seedheads stay standing into winter. One cut in late winter, and the garden returns fresh each spring.

This is the heart of the pitch. Three ideas only: structure, drifts, standing through winter.

The winter point is the one people don't expect. It's also what makes the style look so natural.

The plan

Drifts either side of a winding path.

The brick courtyard and edging stay. A soft path curves through the bed to the bench, so you walk through the planting rather than past it.

Orient her first: house on the left, lawn at the bottom, conifer hedge on the right, bench at the far end.

The plan is interpretive, not a planting plan. Exact plant positions come in the detailed plan after feedback.

Path material isn't confirmed yet. Present it as "a soft path" and take her lead on gravel versus brick.

Layer one

The grasses

The frame of the garden. Upright, airy and never still.

Feather Reed Grass

to 1.8 m in flower
Calamagrostis × acutiflora 'Karl Foerster'

The vertical line. Upright plumes, pink-purple in summer and wheat-coloured by autumn, still standing in winter.

Tufted Hair Grass

45 cm, to 1.2 m in flower
Deschampsia cespitosa

Clouds of purple-green that age to gold and bronze. It glows when the sun is behind it, and it's happy in part shade.

Red Buttons

90 cm
Pennisetum thunbergii 'Red Buttons'

Soft burgundy buttons from midsummer, fading to buff and holding into winter.

Calamagrostis is the vertical rhythm, Deschampsia is the haze, Red Buttons ties the grasses to the burgundy flowers.

Watch-out: Pennisetum as a genus has weedy members. Plan to take seedheads before they shed if self-seeding shows up.

Layer two

Spires of blue and violet

The colour that runs through the grasses for most of the year.

Salvia 'Ostfriesland'

70 cm
Salvia nemorosa

Velvety violet spires from spring into autumn.

Salvia 'Mystic Spires'

60–90 cm
Salvia 'Balsalmisp'

Deep blue spikes from late spring until the cold.

Salvia 'African Skies'

75 cm
Salvia 'African Skies'

Clear azure flowers, spring to autumn.

Salvia 'Waverly'

1.2 m
Salvia 'Waverly'

Pale mauve and white. Flowers nearly all year.

Russian Sage

60 cm
Perovskia 'Silvery Blue'

A smoky lavender haze on silver stems. Loved by bees.

Tall Verbena

1.8 m
Verbena bonariensis

Purple heads floating on see-through stems, so it can sit at the front.

Four salvias gives a long season without looking busy. Waverly is the one that carries flowers through winter.

Verbena bonariensis is the designer's trick: tall but transparent, so it doesn't block the view.

Watch-out: Verbena self-seeds freely. Decide whether we deadhead or let it wander.

The sedums are the autumn and winter story — their dried heads catch frost and hold shape until the late-winter cut.

Black Knight adds depth next to the blues. It behaves like a short-lived perennial and self-sows.

Layer three

A silver thread

Silver and bronze foliage lifts the darker flowers, and most of it smells good when you brush past.

Wormwood

1.2 m
Artemisia absinthium

Finely cut silver leaves. Aromatic, and famously the bitter note in absinthe.

Bush Germander

0.9–1.8 m
Teucrium fruticans

A silver-grey shrub with lavender-blue flowers. Can be clipped to hold edges and corners.

Lamb's Ear

30–45 cm
Stachys byzantina

Soft velvet leaves along the front of the beds. Children will touch it.

Bronze Fennel

1.2–2 m
Foeniculum vulgare 'Purpureum'

Feathery bronze foliage and yellow umbels. The fronds are good in the kitchen.

Germander is the only clipped element. It gives the loose planting a quiet edge where it meets paths or the house.

Watch-out: fennel seeds prolifically around Melbourne. We'd take the seedheads off before they ripen.

Layer four

The groundlayer

Low plants that cover the soil, keep weeds down and soften every edge.

Native Violet Australian native

5–15 cm
Viola hederacea

Round leaves and purple-and-white flowers in the shadier pockets.

Alpine Pratia Australian native

15–30 cm
Pratia puberula

A dense mat covered in small blue stars from late spring into autumn.

Carpet Bugle

15 cm
Ajuga reptans

Glossy bronze rosettes with blue spikes in spring.

Lesser Knotweed

30 cm
Persicaria affinis

Rose-red spikes that fade to pink, and leaves that turn bronze in autumn.

Seaside Daisy

30–60 cm
Erigeron karvinskianus

Tiny pink-and-white daisies from spring into winter.

The two natives do the shade work. Worth saying out loud — it's where the Gardener & Son practice shows up in this garden.

Check in Notion: the knotweed record is titled "amplexicaulis" but the description, height and photos are affinis. The deck uses affinis.

Watch-out: seaside daisy spreads by seed into cracks and paths.

Flowering calendar

Something in flower from September to June.

Read it left to right from spring. The peak is December to April, when almost everything is out at once.

June to August is quiet for flowers, and that's by design: winter is the seedhead season. Waverly keeps a little colour going.

A year in the garden

Four different gardens, one planting.

Spring

Fresh green clumps. Violets, pratia and ajuga at ground level; germander and the first salvias above.

Summer

Full height. Grasses in flower, drifts of blue and burgundy, bees everywhere.

Autumn

Sedums colour up, seedheads turn bronze and gold, and the low sun comes through the grasses.

Winter

Silhouettes and seedheads. Structure, shelter for insects, then one cut to make way for spring.

If there's only time for one image set, the winter one does the most work — it answers "won't it look messy?" before she asks.

Who it feeds
A garden that's busy even when you're not in it.

Bees

Salvias, perovskia, scabiosa and sea holly give nectar and pollen across a long season.

Hoverflies

Yarrow and fennel's flat flower heads suit small insects that help keep aphids in check.

Butterflies

Tall verbena and gaura are landing pads through summer and autumn.

Small birds

Seedheads left standing over winter mean food, perches and insects to forage.

This connects the style to why we do what we do. Leaving it standing isn't laziness, it's habitat.

Measuring the garden

How we'll know it's working.

Biodiversity structure

Four layers of planting, from groundcover to tall grasses, instead of one.

Soil & water

Covered, mulched soil that holds moisture, and hardy plants that need little water once settled.

Habitat complexity

Stems, seedheads and dense low cover left in place for insects and small birds all year.

Connectivity

Flowers from spring to autumn that link this garden to the trees and gardens around it.

Evidence

We record what's planted, photograph it as it grows, and come back to see what arrives.

The same five measures we use for every garden we make.

Keep this light. It's how G&S thinks, not a sales pitch for the Registry. Don't quote a score.

If she asks: these are the five pillars the Registry scores against. Evidence starts on planting day: what went in, and photos as it establishes.

Honest caveat for you: with a mostly exotic palette, this garden won't score strongly on the biodiversity pillar under the current method. Its strengths are habitat structure and the long flowering season.

Looking after it

Less mowing and edging. One big cut a year.

Late winter

The cut

Grasses and perennials cut back close to the ground, all at once. Divide clumps every few years.

Spring

Mulch and fill

A light mulch, and any gaps filled while new growth is still low.

Summer

Water and deadhead

Water deeply through the first summer. Trim salvias and yarrow for a second flush.

Autumn

Leave it standing

No tidying. Take seedheads off only where a plant self-seeds too freely.

Tend

Our garden care visits follow this calendar, so the garden is cut, fed and edited at the right moments. We can talk through what that looks like for Maylands.

Sequence here is real: it's a year. Start at late winter because that's when the garden "resets".

Tend: no pricing on the slide. Talk through visit rhythm if she's interested.
